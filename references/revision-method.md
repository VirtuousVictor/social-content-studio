# Client Review and Revision

Use this method before changing a storyboard, treatment, prompt, edit plan, caption, or production package in response to feedback.

## Parse every note

Record the literal request, likely underlying concern, affected asset, and evidence or approval needed. Do not claim certainty about the user's intent; ask only when different interpretations would lead to materially different changes.

Classify:
- Must change: explicit decision, factual error, legal issue, or approved brand correction.
- Negotiate: requested change could damage the agreed story, clarity, continuity, or schedule.
- Clarify: ambiguous, contradictory, or dependent on missing asset or approval.
- Risk: may affect truth, safety, continuity, timing, cost, or platform fit.

## Translate comments into craft changes

Treat these as diagnostic possibilities, not automatic fixes:
- “Too flat”: sharpen opening conflict, contrast, or rhythm.
- “Too slow”: shorten repeated setup and move proof earlier.
- “Too fast”: reduce shot count or hold key actions and overlays longer.
- “Product unclear”: improve the reveal or show verified use-case proof.
- “Looks artificial”: simplify movement, correct identity/hand/product continuity, and add plausible timing.
- “Not our brand”: restore approved voice, color, visual codes, or product ritual.
- “Not premium”: reduce clutter, clarify light and materials, refine pace and sound.

Preserve approved material. Update only affected deliverables unless a change creates downstream consequences.

## Response and tracking

Summarize the interpretation, concrete proposed action, what stays, what changes, and what still needs approval. For external client wording, acknowledge the concern and describe the fix without exposing internal doubts. Track version, owner, deadline, and unresolved risks.

For a client-facing reply, phrase interpretation as a working understanding rather than a claim about hidden intent. A concise pattern is: “I understand the concern as [observable issue]. I suggest [specific craft change], which keeps [approved element] while addressing [concern]. Please confirm [only the decision that blocks execution].” Prefer “this change affects…” to “we can't do that”; propose an alternative when a requested revision would harm the agreed story, product truth, or continuity.
